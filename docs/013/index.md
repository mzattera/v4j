## Abstract

In a [previous note](..\012\), I have discussed how morphological patterns we see in Voynich words at special position in the text might be the result of "[interesting words](../012/#interesting)" appearing at those positions.
  
In this note, I try to identify formal transformations that applied to such interesting words might explain these patterns.



## Methodology

For this note, the [majority version](https://github.com/mzattera/v4j#ivtff) of the Voynich was used;
only the [cluster](../003/) text in running paragraphs (IVTFF locus type = P0 or P1) is considered,
tokens containing unreadable characters were ignored.

As a first step, interesting words for each specific text position were identified, I then try to define how interesting words differ from words in the standard population; these differences are described as transformations applied to otherwise “non-interesting” words. Finally, these transformations are used to explain patterns highlighted in the abstract above.

The following methodology was consistently applied to each textual position:

1. The “sample” is defined as the set of tokens appearing in a specific locus (e.g. all tokens at beginning of a line).
	
1. “Interesting words” are identified as those word types appearing in the sample significantly more often, compared with the standard population (binomial test, p=0.01).
	
1. Potential transformation candidates were hypothesized (e.g. adding 's' as prefix) that, if applied to words in the standard population, could have resulted in an interesting word. Each interesting word was then tested by reversing the transformation (e.g. removing the initial 's-'); if this resulted in a word appearing in the standard population at least three times, the candidate transformation was retained; otherwise, it was discarded.
	
1. Transformation candidates are further filtered, keeping only those such that 1) the transformation can be applied to at least 2% of the interesting tokens, with a minimum of 3. 2) the transformation can be applied to interesting words significantly more often, compared with words in the standard population (binomial test, p=0.01).
	
To illustrate this process, consider the following example: for the Herbal-A section, the analysis begins by isolating all tokens in the first line of each paragraph, which constitutes the sample (n = 1,293 tokens). Within this sample, certain word types appear significantly more often than in the standard population. These statistically significant types are defined as “interesting words” (n = 685 tokens  - point 1. and 2. above)); an example of interesting word could be 'pCor', appearing 7 times in the sample and never in the standard population. The work hypothesis is that interesting words like this were created by adding ‘p’ to an otherwise “non-interesting” word; the transformation candidate is then “insert ‘p’ at slot 3”. In the case of 'pCor', the word could have been created by adding ‘p’ to the word 'Cor', which appears 6 times in the standard population; the candidate passes the first test (point 3. above).  Finally, we notice that there are several interesting words that could have been generated by this transformation5 (e.g.  'opCy', 'opCol', 'qopCy', 'pCoTy') for a total of 145 tokens and 101 word types; by converse, the standard population has only 12 tokens and 10 word types that could be the result of such transformation (point 4.). Therefore, “insert ‘p’ in slot 3” of existing words is a likely way in which such interesting words in first line of paragraph were created.

This does not imply that the author of the Voynich was applying exactly these transformations when writing the
manuscript, but the transformations are a way to describe how interesting words differs from others. In other terms,
transformations are descriptive, not prescriptive.

I considered  following six transformations; which possibly cover all changes in words that could cause the regularities described in the introduction (e.g. longer/shorter tokens, anomalous character frequencies, etc.):

  * Inserting a specific character at a given slot.

  * Removing a specific character from a given slot.

  * Substituting a specific character in a given slot with another admissible character for that slot.

  * Inserting a specific character before a given character in a word (at any slot).

  * Adding a one-character prefix to word beginning with a given character.

  * Adding a one-character suffix to word ending with a given character.
  
The results of this analysis for each text position are presented below by section. Although substantial overlap exists, the set of transformations is never completely identical across all sections.
An entry such as:  

```
Insert 'p' in slot 3 [146, 102] 
```

indicates that the specified transformation (inserting 'p' in slot 3) applies to 146 tokens and 102 word types within the subset of identified interesting words.
Summary tables are presented in the following format:

```
 Population   Interesting
Tokens Types  Tokens Types
01,3%  03,1%  35,2%  34,4%
```

For each section, these tables report the proportion of tokens and word types—both in the standard population and within the set of interesting words—that can be accounted for by the listed transformations (e.g., in the example above, 35.2% of interesting tokens are explained by at least one of the listed transformation). These coverage rates are typically an order of magnitude higher for interesting words than for the standard population. This discrepancy demonstrates that the transformations do not merely reflect general morphological features of the text, but rather highlight a distinct pattern unique to interesting words, which in turn accounts for their statistical deviation from the baseline population (e.g. this specific transformation will account for longer tokens and a higher frequency of ‘p’).
